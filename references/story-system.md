## 1. Product truth brief

Write this compact brief before layout work:

| Field | Required answer |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who is deciding, and in what context? |
| Traffic source | What brought this visitor here, and what promise preceded the page? |
| Awareness | What does the visitor already understand about the problem and product? |
| Existing behavior | What are they doing immediately before using the product? |
| Tension | What goes wrong, takes too long, or remains uncertain? |
| Primary objection | What is the strongest reason they may not believe or choose the product? |
| Mechanism | What does the product actually do? |
| Proof | What real artifact can demonstrate the mechanism? |
| Outcome | What becomes measurably or visibly different? |
| Conversion | What is the single next action? |
| Claim sources | Which file, product state, supplied artifact, or approved source supports each claim? |

Reject unsupported superlatives. If proof is not available, demonstrate the
mechanism honestly instead of manufacturing social proof.

## 2. Scene matrix

Connect every scene to a visitor question, factual source, and fallback before
layout work begins:

| Scene | Visitor question | Claim and source | Evidence | Visual object | Motion purpose | Mobile fallback | Reduced-motion state | CTA relevance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use only the rows the product supports. A motion purpose must state what the
visitor should notice or understand. "Add energy" is not enough. Remove a row
when it repeats a claim, lacks a source, or exists only to fill space.

For a focused refactor, create rows only for affected scenes and record the
existing behavior that must remain intact.

## 3. Choosing a protagonist

A protagonist gives continuity to the page. Choose something already native to
the product:

- developer tool: command, diff, build, request, trace, deployment;
- workflow product: task, approval, document, handoff, decision;
- data product: event, query, row, signal, forecast;
- creative tool: prompt, frame, draft, asset, revision;
- marketplace: request, match, listing, dispatch, transaction;
- consumer product: message, habit, moment, route, collection.

The protagonist should be:

- recognizable in one glance;
- capable of changing state;
- connected to real product behavior;
- easy to render with type, DOM, SVG, or an existing product asset.

Do not invent a metaphor first and force the product into it.

## 4. Scene roles

### Hook

**Question:** Why should the visitor keep scrolling?

Show one protagonist, one promise, and one primary CTA. Use a single `h1`.
Avoid explaining the full architecture here.

Useful interactions:

- typed or assembled protagonist;
- short text entrance;
- restrained atmosphere;
- exit motion that hands the protagonist to the next scene.

### Momentum

**Question:** What is already happening?

Show the visitor's workflow in motion. Build pressure with sequence, not alarmist
copy.

Useful interactions:

- a traveling object;
- a track or path;
- statements passing through a spotlight;
- layered timing that establishes speed.

### Tension

**Question:** What concrete failure or gap matters?

Prefer three specific examples over a broad feature grid. Present them as
evidence, not decoration.

Useful interactions:

- exhibits or annotated artifacts;
- progressive disclosure;
- state markers;
- a narrowing visual focus.

### Interruption

**Question:** What does the product do at the critical moment?

Demonstrate the mechanism. This is normally the page's most controlled scene.

Useful interactions:

- a barrier, checkpoint, comparison, interception, merge, or transformation;
- deterministic rows or state transitions;
- one decisive time-based impact;
- an opaque transition into a new visual world.

### Proof

**Question:** Why should the visitor believe the claim?

Use real output whenever possible:

- command output;
- before/after product state;
- receipt, trace, audit record, or exported file;
- annotated screenshot;
- reproducible inputs and outputs;
- a short interactive demonstration.

Allow the document to breathe. Proof benefits from normal scrolling and
readable detail.

### Compounding value

**Question:** Why is this more valuable after repeated use or at scale?

Show memory, reuse, accumulation, collaboration, history, or network effects.
Do not force this scene if the product has no compounding mechanism.

### Differentiation

**Question:** Why can this product provide the outcome structurally?

Contrast mechanisms rather than insulting competitors. One precise sentence is
stronger than a comparison grid built from unverifiable claims.

### Resolution

**Question:** What outcome does the visitor now want?

Echo the interruption scene in its successful state. Then present the primary
CTA and one optional secondary action.

## 5. Evidence hierarchy

Prefer evidence in this order:

1. Live or reproducible product behavior.
2. Real product output or stored artifact.
3. Annotated product UI.
4. Verified customer result or testimonial.
5. Mechanism diagram grounded in implementation.
6. Plain factual explanation.

Avoid fake terminal output, fabricated avatars, invented logos, and placeholder
metrics. Mark prototypes or simulated examples as such.

For each artifact, record its source and whether it is current, approved, and
safe to publish. When proof is unavailable, narrow the claim or show the
mechanism directly. Do not turn an unsupported claim into decorative evidence.

## 6. Visual metaphor tests

Keep a metaphor only if it passes all five tests:

1. **Native:** it arises from product behavior or audience vocabulary.
2. **Legible:** visitors understand it without a paragraph of explanation.
3. **Extensible:** it can support at least three scenes without becoming a
   costume.
4. **Functional:** it helps explain state, causality, or proof.
5. **Original:** it does not merely reproduce the reference site's identity.

If it fails, return to the product's literal objects.

## 7. Copy rules

- Lead with nouns and verbs native to the product.
- Keep the hero promise short enough to remember.
- Pair every large claim with nearby proof or mechanism.
- Use short statements during high-motion scenes.
- Use longer explanation only after the visual climax.
- Give technical users exact commands, states, files, or outputs when true.
- Remove sentences that could appear unchanged on a different product's site.
- Avoid urgency, fear, or hype that the product cannot support.

## 8. Originality review

Before accepting the concept, ask:

- Does the page still make sense if all animation is removed?
- Could the same copy sell five unrelated products?
- Is color communicating state, or merely decorating empty space?
- Does every section introduce a new idea, or only a new card layout?
- Are the impressive moments tied to product mechanics?
- Is the metaphor the product's, or the reference site's?
- Are there more visual effects than pieces of real evidence?

If the result feels generic, strengthen product truth before adding polish.
